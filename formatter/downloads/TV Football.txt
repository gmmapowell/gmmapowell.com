title=TV Football


What most people don't seem to understand when they talk about football is that there are three completely different sports, all of which masquerade under the name of football.  And then they try and come up with a single set of rules and opinions to cover all three.  Which is, of course, ludicrous.


What, then, are these three distinct sports?


* There is football that is played, week in, week out, by millions of people around the world as a recreational activity.  This ranges from kickabouts in the park or on the beach to organised five-a-side and eleven-a-side leagues with "professional" referees.  Although this covers a wide range, the key factor in this experience is that it is all about the *participants*.  The spectators, if any, are parents, boyfriends or girlfriends of the players.
* There are then semi-professional or professional clubs which attempt to make money - and pay their players - by selling tickets to spectators to watch the game.  The spectators significantly outnumber the participants, and there is a very real sense - since they are footing the bill - that the game should be run for their benefit.
* And then there is "TV football".  A few clubs allow the TV cameras in and then allow the wants and desires of the TV audience to drive what happens on the pitch.  The most obvious of which is VAR - which seems "obvious" to a casual TV watcher, but is hated by anyone who is present.


It makes no sense to me to consider these "the same sport" and I'm not even convinced that it is reasonable to have a single set of rules covering all three formats.


The most obvious difference is in funding.  For participant football, all of the money to cover the costs has to come from the players themselves, and they will generally take advantage of common facilities - such as parks and recreational facilities - that are paid for and maintained by local authorities.  Spectator football has more costs - specifically around players and facilities, but also in order to provide for spectators - ticket sellers, concession stands and security.  These costs are generally met through ticket sales and concessions.  The costs associated with TV football are astronomically higher - but are in fact driven by the inflation brought into the game through TV revenue.  There is no fundamental reason why TV football should not run at a profit - and the only reason it does not do so is that individual clubs are unable to control their costs in a competitive market.


Given this, the way in which football is currently organized makes no sense, because while it has adapted, slowly, to the increasingly influence of TV and its money in the game, it still thinks of itself as an in-person spectator sport and tries to treat the top tier of football as being "the same thing" as the second tier, which it patently is not.


So, what would I do differently?


Football is played around the world, and there are many places around the world where I have no idea what happens, or what would specifically make sense.  So I'm only going to talk about what I do know - which is Europe and, in particular, Britain.  Anybody else in other parts of the world can decide if - and how - to adapt this to their local conditions.


First off, I don't think anything particularly needs to be done about participant football, but I would like to see, as part of a general restructuring, more money being made available to grass roots clubs and an attempt made to extend their reach and encourage more people to be involved.


Spectator football appears to be fundamentally sound, except for one thing - there aren't enough supporters.  At the end of the day, however spectator football goes about raising money for itself, the fact is that the amount of money that any given club can raise is proportional to the number of fans it has.  Completely unscientifically, it seems to me that the break-even point for a club that has to meet all of its expenses is probably around 10,000 - more or less depending on other factors such as their wealth and spending habits.  Most Championship clubs in England manage that; most of the others do not.


What other sources of revenue do they have?  Basically two: they can receive cash injections from generous owners, and they can receive some level of direct or indirect support from other organizations and governments - for example, in terms of providing equipment and facilities.  At the moment, a small trickle of cash makes its way down from the Premier League (about 0.5 million, compared to about 150 million for the Premier League clubs).


At the same time, the costs of running football clubs - and in particular, the cost of paying, and paying for, players - are rising because of the competitive pressures that come from Premier League clubs and their academies.  The average Premier League club can have fifty or more players on its payroll - most of whom never play - while an average League 2 club struggles to afford 25, even paying lower wages.


The obvious solution to this inequality is for the Premier League clubs to hand over a greater proportion of their cash to the teams in lower leagues.  But why should they?  Because they have access to a source of income which is all but denied to the clubs in lower tiers: TV revenue.


TV revenue is out of all proportion to all other revenue that any but the biggest clubs have access to.  It is the TV revenue from the major broadcasters that the Premier League distributes among its member clubs along with the "parachute payments" to clubs relegated the previous season.  This TV revenue comes from the desire of very, very casual football fans - most of whom never actually go to games in person - to be able to watch, from the comfort of their own homes, the "important" matches of the weekend so that they can have the right opinions about them come Monday morning in the office.  And to them, paying #20 - #50 a month for the privilege of watching 3 or 4 games a weekend is well worth it - and certainly good value compared to buying tickets to go to the games.  Not to mention the fact that there is nothing like the capacity in Premier League grounds for that many people to go.


At the same time as benefiting only the top clubs, meeting this need for very casual fans also sucks much of the oxygen out of the spectator game.  The games that are discussed in offices and schools are the big, *national* games, not the local games that they could have gone to.  The coverage in newspapers and online follows the same pattern.


In my view, this reality needs to be acknowledged and commercial football needs to be reorganized to reflect this reality.  We need to ask "what football should be on TV and when?", "how should it coexist with spectator football?" and "can we get more fans to go to games?"


Ironically, I think the solution to these problems is to create a pan-European league, not dissimilar to the much-derided European Super League proposed in the summer of 2021.  Before you howl, hear me out.


For all of the criticisms of the European Super League, the fact is that the real objection to it was the fact that it represented both a power and a money grab by the largest clubs.  My proposal exactly reverses this: the main objective of my pan-European League would be to generate money to put back into the domestic leagues while simultaneously satiating the demand for the very casual fan and stimulating demand to attend games among more serious fans.


So, first off, let me outline the structure of the Pan-European League (PEL).  It would have 3 divisions: a premier division of 20 clubs and two parallel secondary divisions of 20 clubs each.  Basically, these would be the 60 biggest clubs in Europe, who would all leave their current domestic leagues to take part.  There would be no automatic promotion or relegation between the PEL and domestic leagues, but teams that finished in the bottom places of the secondary divisions would be subject to ejection if the league felt they were not sufficiently competitive.
