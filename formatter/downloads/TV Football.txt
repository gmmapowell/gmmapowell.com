@Article
title=TV Football


Back in 2021, uproar was caused when a &sp
&link 'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Super_League' 'European Super League'
&sp was proposed.  While I was not in favour of the precise scheme being proposed, I did think that the basic an idea - a pan-European competition between some group of "the best teams" made sense.  Let me try and explain why.


What it's easy to miss is that there are three completely different sports, all of which masquerade under the name of football.  And then they try and come up with a single set of rules and opinions to cover all three.  Which is, of course, ludicrous.


What, then, are these three distinct sports?


* There is football that is played, week in, week out, by millions of people around the world as a recreational activity.  This ranges from kickabouts in the park or on the beach to organised five-a-side and eleven-a-side leagues with "professional" referees.  Although this covers a wide range, the key factor in this experience is that it is all about the *participants*.  The spectators, if any, are parents, boyfriends or girlfriends of the players.
* There are then semi-professional or professional clubs which attempt to make money - and pay their players - by selling tickets to spectators to watch the game.  The spectators significantly outnumber the participants, and there is a very real sense - since they are footing the bill - that the game should be run for their benefit.
* And then there is "TV football".  At the top of the tree, there are a few clubs which broadcast their games on TV in exchange for much more cash than the value of tickets.  The TV companies recoup their money by selling advertising.  These games, then, need to be run for the benefit of advertisers who are only interested in how many people are watching the broadcast.


It makes no sense to me to consider these "the same" sport and I'm not convinced that it is reasonable to have a single set of rules covering all three formats.  Confusion always reigns in debates about how football should be run and governed because people propose solutions that apply to one format but not to the others.  


The most obvious difference is in funding.  For participant football, all of the money to cover the costs has to come from the players themselves, and they will generally take advantage of common facilities - such as parks and recreational facilities - that are paid for and maintained by local authorities.  Spectator football has more costs: around players and facilities; and to provide for spectators - ticket sellers, concession stands and security.  These costs are generally met through ticket sales and concessions.  The costs associated with TV football appear astronomically higher - but are in fact driven by the inflation brought into the game through TV revenue.  There is no _fundamental_ reason why TV football should not run at a profit - and the only reason it does not do so is that, looked at objectively, each individual club has a gambling addiction: spending just a little more money will "guarantee" them better results, which will generate more income, which will make them richer.  This is a strategy, but it is guaranteed to fail for most clubs in a zero-sum game for the fixed set of participants.


Given this, the way in which football is currently organized makes no sense, because while it has adapted, slowly, to the increasingly influence of TV and its money in the game, it still thinks of itself as an in-person spectator sport and tries to treat the top tier of football as being "the same thing" as the second tier, which it patently is not.


So, what would I do differently?


Football is played around the world, and there are many places around the world where I have no idea what happens, or what would specifically make sense.  So I'm only going to talk about what I do know - which is Europe and, in particular, Britain.  Hopefully there is enough context here for anybody in other parts of the world to interpret this in their own context.


First off, I don't think anything particularly needs to be done about participant football, but I would like to see, as part of a general restructuring, more money being made available to grass roots clubs and an attempt made to extend their reach and encourage more people to be involved.  Of course, it could just be that since I'm not involved, I am not seeing the problems at that level.


Spectator football appears to be fundamentally sound, except for one thing - there aren't enough supporters.  At the end of the day, however spectator football goes about raising money for itself, the fact is that the amount of money that any given club can raise is proportional to the number of fans it has.  Completely unscientifically, it seems to me that the break-even point for a club that has to meet all of its expenses is probably around 10,000, assuming a ticket price of £20 or so. Most Championship clubs in England manage to do this; most of the others do not.


What other sources of revenue do they have?  Basically two: they can receive cash injections from generous owners, and they can receive some level of direct or indirect support from other organizations and governments - for example, in terms of providing equipment and facilities.  At the moment, a small trickle of cash makes its way down from the Premier League (about 0.5 million, compared to about 150 million for the Premier League clubs).


At the same time, the costs of running football clubs - and in particular, the cost of paying, and paying for, players - are rising because of the competitive pressures that come from Premier League clubs and their academies.  The average Premier League club can have fifty or more players on its payroll - most of whom never play - while an average League 2 club struggles to afford 25, even paying lower wages.


The obvious solution to this inequality is for the Premier League clubs to hand over a greater proportion of their cash to the teams in lower leagues.  But why should they?  Because they have access to a source of income which is all but denied to the clubs in lower tiers: TV revenue.  It may not seem to them to be in their best interests, but the reality is that _however much income they have_, they will always end up gambling it away, because there are a finite number of "best payout spots" and a finite number of "the best players".  In order to get the payout, they need to pay for the players.  It's as simple as that.  Taking their money away _before_ they can fritter it away will not affect their calculations at all; it will reduce the inflated salaries that their players are paid, but ultimately that will end up going to players down the league who are paid less.  It's not quite "everyone wins", but it's close.


TV revenue is out of all proportion to all other revenue that any but the biggest clubs have access to.  It is the TV revenue from the major broadcasters that the Premier League distributes among its member clubs along with the "parachute payments" to clubs relegated the previous season.  This TV revenue comes from the desire of very, very casual football fans - most of whom never actually go to games in person - to be able to watch, from the comfort of their own homes, the "important" matches of the weekend so that they can have the right opinions about them come Monday morning in the office.  And to them, paying £20-£50 a month for the privilege of watching 3 or 4 games a weekend is well worth it: it certainly represents good value compared to buying tickets to go to the games.  Not to mention the fact that there is nothing like the capacity in Premier League grounds for the same number of people to go in person as can watch on TV.


Not only does this only provide benefits for the top clubs, meeting this need for very casual fans also sucks much of the oxygen out of the spectator game.  The games that are discussed in offices and schools are the big, *national* games, not the local games that they could have gone to.  The coverage in newspapers and online follows the same pattern.


In my view, this reality needs to be acknowledged and commercial football needs to be reorganized to reflect this reality.  We need to ask "what football should be on TV and when?", "how should it coexist with spectator football?" and "can we get more fans to go to games?"


Ironically, I think the solution to these problems is to create a pan-European league, not dissimilar to the much-derided European Super League proposed in the summer of 2021, but organized for the benefit of all European football clubs, not just the ones broadcast on the TV.


For all of the criticisms of the European Super League, the fact is that the real objection to it was the fact that it represented both a power and a money grab by the largest clubs.  My proposal exactly reverses this: the main objective of my pan-European League would be to generate money to put back into the domestic leagues while simultaneously satiating the demand for the very casual fan and stimulating demand to attend games among more serious fans.


So, first off, let me outline the structure of the Pan-European League (PEL).  It would have 3 divisions: a premier division of 20 clubs and two parallel secondary divisions of 20 clubs each. These would comprise 60 of the "bigger" clubs in Europe, who would all leave their current domestic leagues to take part.  There would be no automatic connection between the domestic leagues and the PEL.  But it would not simply be the biggest clubs by revenue, but as with the current structures of the Europa League, it would be important to try and include at least one club from each country or region of Europe.  While it might be the case that these would languish in the third tier, it would give the residents of that region a club to rally around, and the opportunity to see clubs from other regions come and visit.  And as with all things football, every season would offer the prospect of being one of the teams to win promotion.


All the PEL games would obviously be televised.


A key factor for me would be that the PEL should not "cannibalise" the participant or spectator sports.  That being the case, the schedule should be such that it does not clash with the regular timings of domestic leagues.  Ideally, in my view, that would mean that *no* PEL games would be played on weekends anywhere in Europe.  If that is not practical, then they should not be played on, for example, Saturdays if involving clubs from England or Sundays for clubs from Spain.


Instead, multiple games should be played every weeknight.  I would leave it up to the broadcasters to decide exactly how many games they think they can "get away with", but given there are 20 teams in each league, two games a night, five nights a week in each league seems the obvious solution.  Given that the intent here is just to provide content for the casual fan on TV, it does not seem unreasonable to play at a slower pace than this, but obviously you need to get through the season at some point.  I'm sure some marketing guru will also come up with some plan such as "Super Wednesday" when 20+ matches are played at the same time (just making sure not to cannibalise the schedule for the other four nights).


The game can also be organised for the benefit of TV.  If they want things like VAR, time-outs, tactical breaks, whatever it may be, they can say that is part of the deal.


What if no supporters want to attend the matches in person?  It's possible, although unlikely.  I would think that if nothing else, there would be a lot of casual fans who would want to go to the occasional game, but if not, all the revenue is coming from TV, so lower the prices for fans to attend.  If necessary, _pay_ fans to attend, in free beer if nothing else.  If that still doesn't get the fans in, the TV companies will need to decide if they want to change direction or just play without fans.  We did it during COVID, after all.


This would also help rebalance the domestic leagues.  By removing all the biggest clubs from each national league, they would become more open.  It would also remove all of the excessive European competition money from the domestic games.  There should also be a "tax" on all of the TV revenue (25%?) that is handed to the domestic leagues.


Domestic games could continue to be televised.  They obviously wouldn't attract the same audience that the PEL games would; but the coverage here should be sensitive to the context, rather than making the rules.  That is, things like VAR should not be permitted, and the presenters should not be as interested in controversy and criticizing the officials, but recognize that they are doing their job.  It would also be good if, during the course of a weekend, a number of games from across the pyramid were selected for broadcast.


Whether any of this would encourage people to participate, or go out and watch their local teams, I do not know.  But hopefully by providing both oxygen to the domestic game and money to fund it, there would be the opportunity for a whole new generation to discover the pleasure of standing on the terraces and supporting your local team.  You never know, that might be the game discussed in the office on Monday morning.
