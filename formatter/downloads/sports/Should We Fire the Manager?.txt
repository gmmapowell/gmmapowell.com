@Article
title=Should We Fire the Manager?
saveAs=sports/fire-the-manager


In what seems a recurring ritual, managers of sports teams are fired with regularity.


In the English football league, there are &sp
&link 'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_current_Premier_League_and_English_Football_League_managers' '92 clubs with managers'
.  At the current time of writing, 9 of them have served for less than a month, 12 over three years, and the median appears to be about a year.  The obvious question is why any team would sign a contract with a manager for more than a year, but I have discussed contracts elsewhere.


Here, the question I have is: what do you hope to achieve?


The first and most obvious point is that the manager is not _literally_ out on the pitch performing - or failing to perform.  It is apparent why people argue that one of the players should be replaced - particularly a player with a prominent role such as a goalkeeper in football or a quarterback in the NFL.  If a key player is failing to do their job week in, week out, then it makes sense to replace them.


But only if it's an upgrade.


The acting of _firing_ someone by itself rarely makes a situation better (as an aside, I have worked on teams with what I call "negative productivity people" - people who contribute so little, and suck in so much that simply removing them _literally_ improves productivity and morale amongst the rest of the team).  Generally, it is the act of _replacing_ someone with someone better that makes the difference.


But how do you measure that?  Can you even measure that?


It seems obvious that there are metrics to tell you how players are performing.  An NFL QB throws a certain number of passes, and it is easy to measure that and to measure both the percentage of them that are "complete" and the average gain on each throw.  In simple-minded fashion, it is therefore possible to produce a list sorted by these averages.


Except, of course, that each QB is working with different receivers and a different level of protection.  I don't know if it is possible to factor all that in to a single numeric rating, but I know that if I had the job of deciding if I wanted to replace my QB, I would give it a good go before making a decision, qualitatively if not statistically.


And that is probably the easiest decision to make in sports.  Consider a striker in football or a winger in rugby.  Before using "how many times do they score?" as a metric, you need to ask yourself, "how many times are they given the ball?" and "how many times does that happen in a scoring situation?".  Football statisticians now have xG to measure that, but how reliable is it, and how much does it take into account the individual circumstances of how a player plays, and where he excels?


And all of that pales in comparison to assessing the role of a manager.


What does a manager do?  Well, first off, they are generally responsible for obtaining and recruiting players.  Then they are responsible for figuring out how they will play.  And selecting a team.  And deciding on tactics.  And training the players.  And inculcating a sense of mission.  Analyzing performances.  Providing feedback.  Helping the players to understand what worked and what didn't.  Helping them to see what is happening on the pitch and sort out problems on the fly.  And then, during the game itself, making tactical changes and substitutions as appropriate.  Do you think you can put that into one number?  And then say "we should replace this person with that other person?"


Over on Wall St, fund managers are measured against an index (so called "alpha").  When times are good, you can do almost anything and make money.  When times are bad, no matter what you do, you will probably lose money.  So the question is, how do you perform compared to your peers?


But that's a nice, easy, numbers game.  You pick two moments in time (generally when the fund manager started and now) and take the value of the fund, divide it by the value of the benchmark index at both of them.  Alpha is then the ratio between these two: above 1, and the manager has beaten the market.  It should go without saying that on the whole you would expect 50% of managers to be above average and 50% below average.


You could argue that you can do exactly the same thing in sports: the top half of the table has good managers and the bottom half has bad managers.  Except, again, Wall St is fairly much a level playing field: some managers will have cost and scale advantages, and some will have advantages in terms of their staff, resources, contacts and research, but everybody has access to buying and selling the same stocks at roughly the same prices.  Sports teams are anything but: there are differences in players, salaries and all other costs.  The difference in budget between the top and bottom teams in the English Premier League are phenomenal.  No manager could compensate for that (and any who could would, of course, immediately be acquired by a team who could pay more).


So, how _do_ you evaluate a manager?  I think it's a very hard task, and the only people who are probably qualified to do it are people that interact with him or her on a daily basis.  The obvious benchmark is against their own predictions: if the manager says "with this squad, we should be top six" and the team is languishing in 12th, they need to account for that difference.


I also think that even the ambition to bring it down to a single number by which all managers could be ranked is to go about it the wrong way: it is a complex and many-faceted job and some managers will be better than others at some parts of it, while being less good at others.  In part, who the right manager for a job is at any given time may well depend on what the team _needs_ at that time: more training, more cohesion, more confidence.


In short, it comes down to _fit_ which is incredibly hard to measure.


So, assuming you have decided that your manager is not up to the job, should you fire them?


Well, first off, just because they are a manager and a coach doesn't mean they don't need coaching in their _own_ role.  Managers need to learn and improve just like anybody else.  An interesting development in Southern Hemisphere rugby has been the rise of "
&link 'https://www.rugbypass.com/news/leon-macdonald-lands-first-super-rugby-job-since-all-blacks-exit/https://www.rugbypass.com/news/leon-macdonald-lands-first-super-rugby-job-since-all-blacks-exit/' 'coaching consultants'
".  I'm too far removed from the process to understand exactly what they do, but when I see a hole and a peg, I assume I'm not too far off in saying that the obvious function of the role is to be someone on the ground and in the daily mire who understands what is involved in the job of coaching but does not have _responsibility_ for it.  They are therefore hopefully able to observe what the coach is doing - and how the team are reacting - with both objectivity and expertise.  And then talk to the coach about possible alternative strategies, in much the same way a management consultant would work with an executive team.  Improving your current manager - or helping his interaction with the team - might well be more efficient that trying to find somebody else.


Come to that, who's available to take the job?  If you fire your current manager, then you presumably need to go and hire someone else, so you need to look at who's available.  This is an absolute minefield.  If you are one of the richest teams, it's possible that you can go and find someone somewhere else who is being successful and lure them away with lots of cash.  But that assumes that they will be successful in your environment - never a guarantee when the reason that they "seem" successful might well be that they are a perfect fit for the current, different, environment.


For everybody else, you only have the choice of people who are out of work.  Almost by definition, an existing manager who is out of a job is one who has been fired from his last job - therefore a failure.  Any manager who has not had a previous job is, by definition, untested and therefore a risk.  From time to time there will be managers out there who have quit there previous job on a high (Jurgen Klopp in 2024 for example), but these will be few and far between.


There is another category which I would like to see be a more common pathway.  In every job, there is much to learn, and every manager and coach has assistants.  It is obviously possible to pluck one of these assistants out of an organization and promote them to be your manager.  In my view, it would be good if all fired managers then went back to being assistants.  Without the front-line pressure of being "the main man", they could hone skills that were lacking in the previous role and led to their ousting.  Such an attitude could allow them to grow and become more likely to succeed.


In short then, the answer to the headline question is "almost undoubtedly not".  The big question is: what is wrong with the team and how can we fix it?  The only valid reason I see to fire the incumbent is because the answer to that question ends with "and this manager cannot fix that".  In that case you know who you need to hire: someone with those specific skills.  In almost all other situations, don't fire the manager, give them what they need to fix the problem: better players or better assistants and consultants.
