I've just finished reading &sp
&link 'https://www.barnesandnoble.com/w/not-everything-counts-but-everything-matters-ivan-cleary/1145822370?ean=9781460717936' 'Not Everything Counts but Everything Matters'
&sp by Ivan Cleary.  It's an amazing book. (As far as I can tell, it's not available in the UK, so I bought the ebook from Barnes && Noble in the US.)


For those (like me) who don't know, Ivan Cleary is a coach down under for a Rugby League team in a place (Penrith) that I'd never even heard of.  This is by way of saying that I'm not raving about his book because I'm a fanboy, the way I might fetishize something Bruce Springsteen or Warren Gatland had written.  This is very definitely on the merits.


I first heard about the man and the book during a Rugby _Union_ broadcast last autumn.  They were talking about how the book had just been published and how the (All Blacks) team had been discussing the lessons during training.  Something that the _All Blacks_ found valuable to read about professional sports had to be worth reading.  And then the title: _Not Everything Counts but Everything Matters_.  I can't be sure, but I think that grasping this title instinctively is a dividing line between "people like me" and "people not like me".  If you are sitting there wondering what the difference is between things that _count_ and things that _matter_, then you will probably think I'm off my trolley writing this and you probably won't appreciate the book.


In sports, what _counts_ is winning.  And there is a theory that _all that matters_ is winning.  Not so.  And it's really good to see someone say so forcefully enough _that they put it on the cover_.  Since I don't coach professional sports, let's just say that there is a lot of material in the book which has to do with rugby league: a sport I have never played, do not understand, and have only watched a couple of times.  It's fair to say when I watch a game, I have no idea what is going on and I find it boring.  But the lessons here about teamwork and organisations are universal, and to dismiss the book as irrelevant to me "because it's about sports" is a bit like dismissing a book on building trains "because I write software".  You have to look at the details to see the big picture.


The first thing about the book (apart from the title) that grabbed me was the flow.  It's divided into four sections: culture, leadership, the game and winning.  It is never _explicitly_ stated, but there is an obvious flow here: culture leads to leadership shows up in how you play the game which leads to winning.  And another thing that is at best stated obliquely is that _winning is not about being better than the other team_ or making them bad.  It's about being _as good as you can be_.  And that is a universal for those of us who _don't_ see ourselves as being in competition all the time.


And then, time and again, I was drawn in by what he had to say.  First up, "everything matters".  No matter how much it might not seem relevant, everything you do, or don't do, everything you give your oh-so-valuable attention to, how you show up, how you behave, _it all matters_.  I have worked in so many organizations where turning up to meetings on time was "optional".  It's not.  It _matters_.  Why does it matter?  Because if you are not on time, you are saying two things: firstly, that the meeting is less important than you are; and that it is fine for other people to waste their time waiting for you.  It disrespects the meeting and it disrespects your team.  More than once, Ivan makes the point that respect and trust are _vital_ to the success of a team and they _matter_.  If you don't do the things that _matter_, you won't succeed when it comes to doing the things that _count_.


Next up, "The Attraction of Consistency".  You are speaking my _language_, bro.  It's great if you can do something well once.  It's fantastic to be amazing "when it counts".  But I'm sorry, that just doesn't cut the mustard for me any more than it does for Ivan.  My football team (Oldham Athletic) just won promotion back to the football league somehow.  I'm _happy_ that we did, but I neither feel we deserved it nor that I can really believe it.  We were lucky.  What we weren't was _consistent_.  I don't know why.  I'm not close enough to the day-to-day detail to know.  But I _do_ know that if I had been there, I would have been harping on about that day after day.  I do with teams I work on as well.  You get good results not by being amazing, or pulling an all-nighter "when it counts" but by doing all the little things well (testing, refactoring, talking to clients, planning, reflecting, collaborating, checking in on each other) on a _daily_ basis.  Why is this so hard to understand?


And on and on.  He talks about the importance of clear communication; telling the truth; being authentic; being compassionate; being vulnerable.  He talks about mental health, not from a "snowflake" perspective but the genuine article: being there for others and having others be there for you.  I could fill 1,000 words here by just cherry-picking quotes from his book, but I won't.


And Ivan draws the "right" conclusion: this all builds up into a _culture_.  A culture where these things are so expected, so part of the fabric of daily life that it becomes impossible to _not_ do those things.  You would stand out like a sore thumb.


And from there, he looks at leadership and I think he has reached the same place that I'm at: a leader is by definition someone who _does_ things and others _follow_ them.  A manager is not a leader, and a coach cannot be a leader.  A leader most be a _doer_.  And someone who does things in a way that other people look at and emulate.  A leader does not tell _other people_ what to do; that simply doesn't work.


And then, finally, he gets to the things that count: games and winning.  But always through the lens of "how did you train?", "how did you prepare?".  I remember my 'O'-level Maths teacher telling us that "the exams are the easy part; you've worked hard all year to get to this point; now you just need to go in there and show that you were paying attention".  Ivan views games the same way: if you've put the effort in during training and practice you will be the best version of you out there on the pitch.  After that, whether you win or not is out of your hands.  But you know there is nothing _within your control_ that you could have done differently to change the outcome.  As he says, _que sera, sera_.


It might seem that this last thing does not cross over into the world of software, or any other business activity, but that is not so.  It really all comes down to the title: every detail matters; and if you nail all the details, the big picture will take care of itself.  Do the little things right, and you cannot fail to do the big things right.


In other words, I think this book is excellent because Ivan agrees with me about almost everything.  And expresses it brilliantly.  If you don't see the world this way, you probably won't enjoy the book.  And I hope we never work together; neither of us would enjoy that, either.
