title=Football FFP Redux

So the basic idea is to put FFP onto a much more sound financial basis; at the same time, I'm planning on addressing a number of other troubling issues around money in football.


Instead of it being an "arbitrary" set of rules, it should be much more embedded into things clubs actually care about: players, squads and bills.


@Section
title=Squad Value and Transfers 


The basic mechanism is to work on the basis of value and rates of return.  In this model, it is assumed that each player has a certain "value" and that all players offer an equivalent rate of return based on their value.  Each team then has a "total squad" value which is the sum of the values of the players in the squad.  For these purposes, the "total squad" is all the players who have signed a professional contract with the club.


FFP simply places a limit on this "total squad value".  For leagues (such as the premier league) which offer an annual grant for participation, it should be a multiple of the amount paid to the club (which obviously varies by club).  If we say the participation grant for a given club is #150m and the multiple is 5, then the maximum total squad value is #750m.  No club may exceed this at any time.


Given that there are two transfer windows in the season, going into each of these, each club may revalue all the players in its squad.  Among other actions, it may "release" players (by setting their value to #0), increase or decrease their value.  Except for the special case of "releasing" a player, each player must meet a minimum set by the governing body.  This exercise is "mark-to-model", in that it attempts to set the players' values based on what the club thinks they are worth.  These changes may be decided (and even published) at any point leading up to the transfer window, but they must be published no later than the COB of the day before the transfer window starts.  Any players NOT listed and valued by this time are assumed to have been released unless special circumstances apply.


The transfer window then offers a "mark-to-market" valuation.  The transfer window is essentially an auction.  Any club may (publicly) offer any amount exceeding the current valuation of any player by at least 5% (which includes "free" for released players) at which point the club has 72 hours to agree, match the offer or receive a better offer (at least 5% better).  If they choose to match the offer, they must accordingly increase the value of that player on their valuation (for FFP purposes, this takes effect at the end of the 72 hour window; for accounting purposes, it is backdated to the start of the transfer window).  If accepting the offer would cause them to exceed the maximum squad value, they must immediately transfer or release another player so that by the time the 72 hour window closes, they are below the maximum squad value.  It is not permitted to reduce the value of a player (other than to zero) during a transfer window.


A team is not required to accept the highest offer it receives; it must simply either accept one of the offers or match the highest offer.


Obviously any team wishing to acquire a player must have the appropriate amount of FFP space available in their squad valuation to make their offer; however, as with the selling club, it is permissible to wait until the end of the 72 hour window to do so.  Again, the only remedies for purchasing clubs to create FFP space are to release or transfer other players; it is not possible to arbitrarily adjust the squad valuations during a transfer window.


If a second offer is received during the 72 hour period a club is considering an offer, it may choose to match the first offer and then have 72 hours from the tabling of the second offer to consider its response to that offer.


If an offer is made less than 72 hours from the end of the transfer window, the club has the additional option to simply ignore the offer and it will void at the end of the window.  However, they also have the option to accept the offer provided they do so before the closing of the transfer window.


This very formal mechanism notwithstanding, there are no limits on the informal conversations between clubs and managers at any point.


@Section
title=Academy Players


Each club may have an academy of younger players.  These players are not counted towards the overall squad value.  They may not be valued or transferred. All players in the academy must be under the age of 18 at the start of a transfer window.  Academy players may play for the senior squad while remaining in the academy.


Players graduate from the academy on the day before the opening of the first transfer window when they would be 18 or over.


Players graduating from the academy may be offered contracts at the club's discretion.  Every player offered a contract must be entered onto the schedule of players and assigned a value at least at the league minimum value for their tier.  Contract offers cannot be extended which would violate the cap on the total squad value.  Players not offered contracts automatically become free agents.


@Section
title=Wages and Taxes


The squad value is also used for various accounting purposes.


First and foremost, it is used to calculate a player's wages.  If we figure that the value of a player is proportional to their contribution to the team, this should be directly reflected in their wages.  Thus, across the leagues involved in the FFP scheme, a player's wages are calculated as a fixed percentage of their value (I recommend 10% but this could be set - even on an annual basis - by the governing body).  As each transfer window comes and goes, the players' wages will be adjusted according to their new valuation.  As noted above, for the purposes of this section, each player's valuation at the end of the transfer window is assumed to be backdated to the start of the transfer window and the wages from the start of the transfer window are paid by the club with whom the player is registered at the end of the transfer window.


Players "released" during the transfer window have their value - and thus their wages - set to zero.  If they are "acquired" by another club, that club must assign them a non-zero value (even if they agreed a zero transfer fee) which must be at least the league minimum, which defines the wages paid by the acquiring club, backdated to the start of the transfer window.  Players may, however, refuse to be transferred on a free, or may not be acquired at all; in these cases, they have the option to leave their current club and become a "free agent".  In this case, they are paid a pro-rata salary for the duration of the transfer window at the rate they were previously entitled to (before the transfer window revaluation).  They may then subsequently sign for any club at any value over the league minimum.


Each club has to pay a "wealth tax" based on the value of its current squad.  Again, this can be set (on an annual basis) by the governing body, but for the sake of argument, let us set it at 15%.  This amount is theoretically due annually but in two instalments (each of 7.5%) based on the value of the squad after the transfer window is complete.  This money is divided (equally?  as determined by the governing body)  into the "grassroots" fund and "redistribution" fund (see below).


Each club also has to pay a "Tobin (Heath) tax" on each of the players it purchases in addition to the actual transfer fee.  Again, the rate of this can be set on an annual basis by the governing body, but should be higher than the wealth tax; let us assume 25%.  This tax has three purposes: it introduces friction into the transfer market; it provides money to the grassroots; it rewards teams for developing young players.


By expressly taxing transfers - as opposed to having expensive squads - it causes clubs to consider more carefully what players they need and if it is worth continually moving players around.  If they decide it is worth it, they can do it, but they have to recognize that there is a depreciation the moment they make a move.  For example, a player acquired "for" #60m goes onto their books immediately at a valuation of #60m, and is paid #6m annually (together with a tax of #9m) but was actually acquired at a cost of #75m.  In other words, the player appears to have depreciated by #15m on purchase.  This deterrent, of course, is mainly "virtuous": by discouraging squad churn, it provides for a more consistent squad over the long term.


The collected tax is then divided into two portions.  Again, the rate of division is up to the governing body, but I suggest a ratio of 4:1.  The larger portion is put into the "grassroots" fund.


The smaller portion is handed to the club whose academy/ies developed the player up to the age of 18.  In the case of multiple academies developing a player up to 18, the fee is divided between the clubs according to a schedule to be published annually by the governing body.


@Section
title=Relegation


When a team is relegated, it will generally find itself in a league which has a lower maximum squad value.  While it must ultimately normalize itself to the environment in which it finds itself, it is given the luxury of two transfer windows to do so.  This is independent of any "parachute" payments that may be paid to the club, and any issues the club may have with making payments to players or taxation.


For these two transfer windows, there are in effect two "maximum squad values" operating in parallel.  The club is allowed to publish its "mark-to-model" squad valuations in accordance with the higher value from the higher league.  While this is in force, it must pay salaries and taxes in proportion to this.  However, it is only able to increase values to match offers during the transfer window - and acquire players - if it can do so while remaining under the lower cap.


In general, it is expected that most clubs will react to this by pricing their players in the first transfer window after relegation with an eye to moving them on while obtaining the maximum return on their investment; the second transfer window will probably see a significant reduction in values to make sure that the players are moved.  Failing that, players will need to be released or significantly marked down prior to the third transfer window (assuming they have not been re-promoted) when they need to meet the new overall squad value.


@Section
title=Pro Forma Accounts


Each club is required to publish on a bi-annual basis a set of "pro forma" accounts which must be certified by their auditors to be "broadly in line" with their actual detailed financial statements.  Each of these must cover the period from the start of a transfer window to the day before the start of the next window and be published no later than a week after the start of the transfer window (subject to revision).  The purpose of these accounts are twofold: to make clear to the governing body the overall health of the club, and to inform the fans of the club about the basic flows of the money in the club.


As usual, there are two statements that are required: a "balance sheet" detailing the club's assets and liabilities; and a "profit and loss" statement which indicates the flow of cash into and out of the club during the period.  The governing body should define the exact categories to be listed, but at least the following should be considered:


Balance Sheet


* Assets on hand (deposit accounts, investments, etc)
* Fixed assets (ground, training facilities, etc)
* Squad value (as of last valuation)
* Non-equity debts


Profit and Loss


* Squad salaries
* Other salaries
* Transfer fees in and out
* The academy (if any) considered as a cost center
* Leases and other perennial expenses
* Taxes (both as described here and "real" taxes)
* Matchday income and expenses
* Other income
** Advertising
** Sponsorship
** Direct payments
* Profit or loss


There are two compliance requirements, one for each of these.  On the assets, it is required that the published squad value does not exceed the assets on hand by a factor of more than 10.  On the cashflow, it is required that a loss (if any) does not represent more than 5% of total cashflow.  (Both these numbers can be adjusted on an annual basis by the governing body).


@Section
title=Charitable Funds


It has been noted above that taxes are paid into the "grassroots" and "redistribution" funds.


The "grassroots" fund is designed to further football at the lower levels.  Each administration (region/country, etc) has its own grassroots fund, administered by the appropriate authority and divided up amongst the "lower" tiers of football.


Annual tax collected and paid into this fund by all the clubs in a given tier is distributed to clubs at lower tiers in the same administration.


The portion of transfer fee paid into the grassroots fund is paid to the administration of the club selling the player to be distributed at its lower tiers.


Where the affected club is at the bottom tier in its jurisdiction, the funds are distributed at that tier.


Once the relevant amounts "below" each tier are determined, it is up to the local administration to determine the appropriate formula by which each club receives money.


The "redistribution" fund operates within a tier.  The money from this fund must be recycled to the clubs within the tier according to a formula agreed by the local administration and the clubs in the tier.  While it is not a requirement that the funds be allocated equally between the clubs in the tier, it is intended that the money be used to try and level the playing field and so it is not acceptable to significantly weight it in favour of the richer or more successful clubs.


@Section
title=Other Benefits


It seems that given this set up, there is no need for players to be signed to "contracts" of a fixed duration.  Each player, on signing for a club, receives a salary package commensurate with the amount for which they were signed.  The club has the option to increase or decrease this at the start of a transfer window but, should they reduce it more than the market considers fair, one or more clubs will swoop to sign the player.  If no clubs do, in fact, make an offer for the player the implication must be that they are not worth more than the owning club says.


In the event of a club and a player reaching an impasse, the club can mark them down to a value of #0, and they will effectively be released; even if they do not leave, their salary will drop to nothing.


It may be considered that a negative side-effect of this is that players may end up with no security should they be injured and unable to play - clubs could simply write them off.  This is true, and probably deserves a special clause - such as clubs must continue to pay a player for a year after they receive an injury that stops them playing.
