title=Just One Mistake
saveAs=life/justOne


Just one mistake may make me doubt everything you say.


By all means, call me intolerant.  But I have two points to make here.  One is that this is not a choice I make, just an honest observation about myself.  The other is that I suspect many other people are just the same but may not recognize it.


Here's an example: during sports events, they have all kinds of statistics up on the screen.  Sadly, they never come with definitions, and generally they don't come with any kind of "constraints" (when *exactly* they were valid, or how subjective they are).  Some are simple counts, some are complicated numbers.  But we are expected to take it all at face value.


But can we, in truth?


The other week I was watching a [Gallagher] Chiefs [SuperRugby] game, and they put up a statistic that said that there had been no scrum penalties so far this game.  Since I had, just a minute before seen a scrum that ended in a penalty, I could clearly see that this statistic (or datum, or factoid as you wish) was incorrect.  I'm not accusing them of lying, or suggesting that they were doing it deliberately; but it remains the case that it was, factually, incorrect.


So when they put up a chart showing possession, territory and "time in opponent's 22" a moment later, I was naturally dubious.  If they couldn't get a simple count of a well-defined fact right, how could they hope to have something as complex as possession or territory correct? (For the record, I still don't know how they define these; for example, when someone kicks the ball away, does the time the ball is in flight count for the kicking team, the team that eventually recovers it or is it just ignored?  Likewise, what if it goes into touch?)


It made me realize that for the rest of the game, I was just implicitly doubting everything they had to say.  My trust in their data collection had been completely broken and there was no real way for them to get it back.  When the number of scrums ending in penalties was next shown (let's say it was 3, but I honestly don't remember now), I had lost track.  Was that a _correct_ 3, including the one that had been "ignored" earlier?  Or had they just always forgotten to count that one, and the correct number was 4?


And if they are going to put up numbers that are sometimes wrong, and thus I am going to doubt all of them, what is the point of putting up any numbers at all?


Pulling back from that specific point, I want to make a more general one.


If we're working together, and you assure me that "such and such" is the case, then the first time I will believe you - you know the code and you know the environment.  I may check (using clarifying questions) that we are saying the same thing, but if you tell me something _is true_, I will believe you.


But woe betide you if you're wrong.


Because after you've told me one incorrect thing - and assured me it is true - I will ever after be forced to accept that you don't know what you're talking about.  And that everything you say needs to be checked.  After a while (maybe 10; maybe more) times when I have wasted time checking things you have told me, you will gain back your reputation as being worth listening to.  But if you were to be wrong again ...


As I consider this, I don't really see that I have an alternative.  I could, of course, be 100% trusting.  But that's just foolish.  I could give you two shots at being wrong before putting you in the "yeah, but no" category.


At the end of the day, it's not dissimilar to &sp
&link 'https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner%27s_dilemma' "Prisoner's dilemma"
&sp except this is the real world and nuanced, rather than being scored.  And so, over time (or "rounds" if you prefer), I will slowly move back to being more accepting of more probable statements - or else statements that seem less significant if you're wrong.
