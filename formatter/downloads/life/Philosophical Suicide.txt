@Article
title=Walking away from Life
saveAs=life/walking-away


I would say that I have been "actively contemplating suicide" for most of my life.  To be more accurate, I have considered it as an option since I was about 12.


For most of that time, I have also been considering the power of language to channel our thoughts.  Probably seminal to this development was Orwell's Appendix on Newspeak in 1984.  Words and phrases have particularly tones and shades.


"Committing suicide" is one such: it suggests a dramatic, sudden act, possibly in the attempt to try and garner attention from an uncaring world.  "Threatening suicide" is another: in spite of how often it's used, how many people actually unpack the "threat" involved here?  It's certainly _not_ the same as threatening to blow up a building (unless that is the chosen means, in which case it is dramatic).


That is not at all what I am talking about here.


So let's try looking at this a different way, and talk about one of life's bigger aspects: work and jobs.  To a first approximation, these four statements are the same:


* I'm going to change job
* I'm quitting my job
* I'm being fired
* My role has gone away


In each case, the current association between individual and wage-earning role is being sundered.  But if you were to have a conversation with somebody, I believe you would act very differently if they opened the conversation with "I'm going to change job" to if they opened with "I'm being fired".  Try a little thought experiment now: go through those four statements and imagine having conversations with other people involving them.  Maybe you're hearing someone else say it: how do you react?  Or maybe you are saying it: what emotions would you channel?  Consider other variations of the same theme and different ways of phrasing it ("I found out this morning that I've been fired"; "I don't have a job anymore").  For all the similarities between what's actually happened here, your emotional response is very different.


I claim it's exactly the same with death.  Ultimately, these statements are all very similar:


* She passed away last week
* He was hit by a car this morning
* She only has three months to live
* He killed himself


They reflect someone dying in the past or future.  Why does the fourth one feel so different? (It isn't to do with timeframe: he is planning to kill himself feels the same, except you may feel the need to intervene.)


I could be wrong, but in my mind the big difference is that "it didn't have to happen".  In most cases when someone dies it is some kind of tragic accident/eventuality/disaster and we can all be sympathetic.  When someone makes a _choice_ to end their life, on the other hand, we instinctively feel that there is "something wrong" and that they either need to seek help, or should have sought help while there was still time.


This may be true in some cases.  It is not true in all cases.


On the flipside of this is the question of growing old.  For as long as I can remember, whenever anybody has bewailed getting old and having a "landmark" birthday, I feel the obligation to ask them to "consider the alternative".  The alternative to getting old is, of course, dying first.  It's the only way to avoid it.  Now, if when you reach 60 or 70 or whatever, your reaction is "hasn't the past decade been great! I can't wait to roll into the next one", I'm not going to say this.  But if you say, "aren't I getting old?" you are, whether you have formed the conscious thought or not, saying there is something bad about being alive _in certain circumstances_, those circumstances being correlated in your mind with your age.


So there comes a point when life "is not worth living".  We already recognise this for individuals who are on life support: we have a process for "turning it off".  We also recognise that there are situations where medical professionals should do no more (commonly cited as "do not resuscitate").  And there is a growing recognition that people with certain terminal or incurable conditions (late-stage cancer, dementia or motor neurone diseases) should be able to choose when to call it a day.


One of the big problems with talking about this is the wildly different perspectives that people have.  As always, such a difference in perspective means that two people, talking across this chasm, are barely having the same conversation.  You may well be a person who comes from the starting point that "life is a gift".  No matter the circumstance, you will say that life is precious.I hear you, and you are entitled to your opinion - at least, as far as it applies to _your_ life.  On the other hand, I come from the starting point that life is basically a trial to be navigated as best I can until I reach the finishing point - death.  I recognize it for what it is: ultimately pointless; and I see "making my own reasons for being alive" as just one more burden put upon me.  I have found in my life things that I love doing; I try and spend the largest proportion of my time I can manage focused on those things.  Other than that, I fritter my time away doing more or less enjoyable things.


Another problem with talking about this is the direct emotional response that many people have: it is a defence mechanism, I think, without which I think humans would never have evolved: life is important; death is to be avoided at all costs.


Hopefully I have said enough to start to close the divide and to calm your emotional response.  Obviously, I don't know where you started and it's possible that you see these things exactly the same way I do.  If so, what follows will be easy.  If not, please try and bear with me and not rush off to get me psychiatric help.  I've talked to professionals about this and they are all happy that I am perfectly sane and have a reasonable and rational - if not common - perspective.


If my life is mine, and mine alone, then when it ends should, logically, be my decision.  There are other factors, such as considering any dependents I may have, but at the end of the day, it should be possible to assume that any rational actor can take those factors into account for themselves.  And if that is not easy for them, then it should be possible for them to go and talk to a counsellor who will help them think through those issues.


But I don't want to consider any of those cases.  I want to talk about just "wanting to leave" and I want to go back to the idea of quitting a job that isn't worth doing anymore.  Maybe you've achieved all you want, or maybe you find the environment toxic.  Maybe it's just soaking up all your time and energy and you don't feel you can put any more effort into it.  So you want to quit.


I claim these have direct parallels in life as a whole, although if you are going to follow me there, you need to park the idea that you are "changing jobs" or "quitting your job to focus on your real life", since obviously neither of those have a parallel with life as a whole.


When I was 12 and contemplating that life wasn't worth living, I came up with one essential principle which kept me going through the rest of my teenage years: procrastination.  This is a decision I can put off until tomorrow/next year/whenever without too much hardship.  (I'm happy if you want to make the case for suicide as a tactical option to get out of something you don't want to do tomorrow; I will listen, but that is not where I'm going: leaving life is final and means that I think that whatever's left is not worth the burden of living it.)  I decided on reviewing every decade whether or not I could see enough promise in what was to come to make it worth continuing day after day.  Until now, that has always come up "keep going", but who knows what the future will hold?


If you've achieved all you wanted to in life, why stick around?  The obvious answer is that "there are always other worlds to conquer", but what if you've reached a point where making that change seems too hard?  What if life is a daily struggle to make ends meet and you can't even sleep at night worrying about all the problems you're running away from?  Well, yes, I would like to live in a world where it wasn't like that too, but for some reason I'm always surrounded by voters who vote for low taxes and punishing the poor and newspapers that make it somehow sound like a struggle for virtue.  In a similar vein, for many people, life may just be too toxic: if all the people left in their life seem to just want to make trouble for them, and everywhere they turn someone is threatening them, why should they not say, "up with this I will not put?".


Or, maybe, long before a physical condition makes life _intolerable_ to some arbitrary standard, you find yourself spending every day battling it.  You could, literally, carry on, possibly even without help, but if you don't want to, _why should other people's morals make you_?


Or maybe you have suffered a loss that you cannot overcome: it might be your spouse of forty years, or one or more children, or just separation from family ties.  Does your life then become an empty vacuum?  If that isn't bad enough, is your mind playing one long "doom loop" of what-ifs and what-could-have-beens?  Is it worth putting up with that for twenty years just to say you lived for another twenty years?  And you are suggesting _I_ am not rational?


If you say, "well, yes, but I would want to carry on in that situation", that is fine.  I say once again, that, as far as I am concerned, that is _your choice_.  My question to you is, why do you feel so self-righteous that you want to deny _me_ - and others - _our_ choice, just because it is different?


What I'm saying is that, at the end of the day, life is basically just like anything else: what you do with it - and specifically, if you want to carry on doing it - is a question of tradeoffs.  Either you think the benefits outweight the costs, or you think the costs outweigh the benefits.  And the only rational advice if the costs outweigh the benefits is to stop.  Only two things make this "more drastic" than in other areas of life: one is the complete irreversibility of it; the other is the fact that there is currently no organised process or agency to make a clean break.  The combination of the societal stigma and the dramatic steps that are generally needed to take your own life make this seem much more radical than it is.


And finally, to change the language, I do not want to talk about "committing suicide" but about "walking away from life".  It has a much gentler feel to it, and more accurately reflects what I think I'm talking about.
