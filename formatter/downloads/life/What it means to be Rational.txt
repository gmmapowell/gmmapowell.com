@Article
title=What it means to be Rational
saveAs=life/rational


From time to time people say to me that they think I'm not rational.  Or, which amounts to the same thing, that what I've said is not rational.  If you feel that way about anything you read here, please let me know.  But first, can we check that we mean the same thing?


The dictionary defines it as "something that is in accord with reason or logic".  In other words, a statement is rational if it can be shown to be an end point of a logical chain of reasoning.  Of course, as any mathematician knows, whether the result you conclude from logic is _true_ or not does not just depend on your grasp of logic, but also whether your basic assumptions are correct.  So please don't tell me I'm being irrational just because you don't agree with me; instead, either tell me my logic is faulty or flawed, or that you disagree with my assumptions.  If you do that, we can go exploring together and see where we end up.


For example, you may believe that immigration is a problem.  If so, you presumably believe that where people were born is something more than an accident of history.  You then conclude that because they were born there, they should stay there.  If they choose to leave and come here, they are acting against human nature.


We will disagree.  You can read my argument elsewhere.  _Both_ are totally rational.


Let me say that again.  Even if we have reached diametrically opposed conclusions, we are _both_ being totally rational.


You see, if you assume that where people are born is _significant_, and I just think it's a thing that happened, then anything and everything that flows from those beliefs will be something we do not agree on.


On the other hand, as it stands, neither of those axiomatic beliefs - mine or yours - is inherently rational.  As stated, we just believe them because we believe them.  _That_ is irrational.  You will find that all over this website.  I believe that all my _arguments_ are rational.  My assumptions are not.  Whenever possible, I have tried to dig deep into my assumptions and tell you where they have come from; every time I do this, I just push further back into my consciousness, justifying one assumption with another.  Ultimately, there is no solid ground on which to base a rational argument: we either fundamentally agree or disagree on some basic axioms.


If you want to tell me my assumptions are wrong, you are welcome to do so - but I will demand a high standard of proof; and beware: since _any_ proof must include some starting assumptions, I can easily tell you that _you_ are being irrational for holding those assumptions.


Only if we can agree on a set of mutually held assumptions can we discuss whether our arguments are rational or not.


@Section
title=Peeling the Onion


I call this whole process - of looking at assumptions, challenging them, and then justifying them - "peeling the onion".  The process is like taking the outer layer off an onion, only to find exactly the same thing inside, repeated all the way down.  Each layer is like a logical argument built on top of another onion.  And you never reach a point at which you can say, "but this is absolute fact".  It is simply an assumption from which you will not be moved.  But if we both do this, it is possible to see more clearly where our paths diverge and why.


_I have plans to build a website $onionpeelers.com$ which will enable people to look at arguments in this way and try and understand their fundamental assumptions.  Like many of my plans, it is still years away._
