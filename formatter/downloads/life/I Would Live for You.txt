title=I Would Live for You


How many times have you heard the expression "I would die for you" (or even an abstract thing).


I think to most people this constitutes "the ultimate sacrifice".  But is it really?  This idea comes, of course, from the fact that once you are dead, all your future possibilities are erased.  In an individual sense, death is the end.  For most of the animal kingdom, then, death is everything.


But for social animals, and especially supersocial animals (such as ants, bees and humans), it is completely different.  We bind into social structures that go way beyond the individual and even the broader sense of (genetic) families: there are wider things that it makes sense for me to give my life, and possibly even my stake in genetic future for.  Because those things may be embedded in the broader culture and my death may represent an opportunity to reinforce those values in the broader culture.


On the other hand, have you ever heard anyone say "I would _live_ for you"?


What does that even mean?  _Obviously_ people want to live.  So why would they do it _for someone else_ when they are clearly the primary beneficiary of being alive?


At the moment, whether to live or die is not an actual choice, apart from dramatic suicide, which also muddies the waters.  So let's enter the world of thought experiments.  Let's imagine that this was _not_ so.


In this scenario, from your twentieth birthday onwards, every five years, you are called in to see a psychiatrist (or someone in that space) and asked a bunch of questions about your life and how you feel about it.  There are three possible outcomes from this conversation:


* You are thoroughly happy with everything, and can't wait to get back to it;
* You are struggling with various issues, and would like to receive help;
* You just want to give up.


The first case is dull, so let's ignore it.  Let's say that the mark of a civilized society is that when you need help you can get it, so the second case is just a double check for people who haven't realized that they need help yet (yes, I know, by this definition there are no civilized societies; completely agree, there aren't).


That leaves us with the third case.  It would seem to me that most people who said "I just want to give up" would be facing something pretty horrendous.  They have had the opportunity to ask for help either at any time in the last five years, or at least in the last hour, when they will have been _specifically asked about this_.  And yet, they still want to give up.


We can now ask the question, not "what help you do need to make life worthwhile?" but "what is there in your life that you want to live for?".  And recognize that the answer may be "nothing".


At that point, the statement "but I would live for X" comes into play.  There may be nothing in my life that really feels like it is rewarding _to me_, but I feel that by being here, I am able to support my family, my friends, some organizations or charities.  When this, that or the other goes away, I will have nothing left I want to live for.


But in the meantime, I would live for you.
