I have written elsewhere about my philosophy of the end of life and, if it isn't already clear, my view is that it is fundamentally up to each individual to choose when they want to leave this life.


In practical terms, the UK Parliament is currently considering a private members' bill to legalize the process of assisting other individuals wishing to walk away from life to do so.  In my view, this is the only humane way to extend the fundamental right to end your life to everyone who wants to take advantage of it.  The problem with suicide is that almost all means of carrying it out are messy, disruptive and brutal, not to mention ringed around with the possibility of failure.  There is no doubt that turning this around so that there is the option to have your death supervised and assisted by another in a clean, dignified environment - with your loved ones around you if it is your mutual wish - would be a massive benefit to those of us who want to walk away from life.


The question then arises about safeguards.  I'm tempted to even ask why they are necessary.  Given that almost everything on the NHS requires joining a waiting list for months surely means that this service would be just the same.  Anyone who is considering it "on a whim" will have six months to reconsider their options.  The bigger problem in my view is the unnecessary suffering that will ensue while people are forced to wait this long after having fulfilled the criteria that they have to be down to their final six months before they can apply.


The argument is made that people can be bullied into by their "loved ones".  Anyone whose loved ones all really want them to die so much that they are prepared to sign up for assisted suicide to avoid arguing with them probably does not have much to live for.  And anyone who doesn't want their children to bully them into suicide so that they can inherit should probably just disinherit their children now.


OK, you're not convinced by any of that.  Let's try something else.


I know, right now, that I don't want to live out my final years in pain, suffering or agony.  In fact, I'll go a lot further and say when I can't actively enjoy life, I'll have had enough.  If I'm housebound, blind, unable to read or program a computer, I do not know what my life is about.  At that point, I would like to walk out.  I'll happily sign an affadavit to that tomorrow.


It seems reasonable to me that the most reasonable safeguard is to allow anyone to write a living will that says either "terminating my life is something I'll consider" or "I would not want to live like this" and then, when the time comes, to say, "look, I've said this for twenty years, let me go".  Anyone who wrote the opposite "I want to hold on to my last breath" would be taken at _their_ word and no amount of bullying or greedy relatives would be able to make them change their minds.  Everybody else would have to take their chances.


Which brings me to the second key point.  The current bill does not, in fact, recognize choosing the time and manner of your death as a right.  It merely acknowledges that a few people in dire straits have that right.  Personally, I would just acknowledge it as an inalienable right and leave it at that: when you feel the time is right, sign up and within a few months your number will be called.


But I am a reasonable man and prepared to compromise.  I would see the following four cases as "good enough" reasons to give to request an empowered early departure from this life:


* That you have a terminal, painful illness and you just don't want to carry on - but there is no need to be in extreme agony or to be in the last few months of life;
* That you have a degenerative disease (dementia, or MND, or whatever) and you don't like what you are becoming and would rather walk away;
* That you have been in a loving relationship with a spouse or partner for 25 years or more and, subsequent to their death, feel that life is overwhelming;
* That, for any reason or no reason, you feel that it is not worth carrying on and, after a course of therapy or counselling lasting at least six months you have obtained the consent of the counsellor that there is no reason to deny you this step.


The question is also raised of "who will be prepared to help people commit suicide?"  or even what they think they mean "where will we find doctors prepared to murder people?"  I don't know what is involved, but I do know that if it is simply a question of volunteering to help people escape from their terminally horrible lives and offer them some dignity and decency in passing, I, for one, would be more than happy to sign up to give them a final injection or whatever it might be.  It may be the case that any such role would require more years of training than I would be prepared to put in, but I suspect that the systems could be designed well enough that operating them would require just a week or two of training.  I could do that, and I'm sure thousands of others could too.  What is holding most people back from imagining this is the question "how can you kill someone?" failing to realize that by the very context the actual question is "are you prepared to lovingly help someone to move on?"  If that doesn't convince you, ask a vet how they cope with killing cute, fluffy animals when their owners ask them to, the heartless b*******s.
