@Article
title=Planning a Garden
saveAs=tech/garden


I believe that there is an old saying along the lines that if you plant a vine today, your children will profit from it; if you plant an olive tree, your grandchildren will thank you for your foresight.


I can't find a reference to this anywhere, but the point is that plants (and particularly trees) take a long time to develop, grow and mature: but when they do, the rewards last a long time.


My son has recently bought a house, and with that comes a garden, and that requires you to "do something" with it.  But what?  It feels a huge and daunting problem at first sight: there is so much land and it's essentially a blank canvas.  Where do you start, and what do you do?


The most important thing (at least for my purposes here) is that this will all evolve _over time_:  all plants require a certain amount of time to develop, but different plants require different amounts of time.  You can sow annuals now (I am writing this in April, so it is absolutely true right now, but even in November you can plant some varieties) and expect them to flower in a few weeks or months; or you can plant bedding plants and have some instant colour; if you want to plant bushes or trees, you can do that, but you will need to expect to wait quite a bit longer for the payoff.


So if you look at the medium term, say two to three years, what should you do _now_ to get the results you want _then_; and then, in the shorter term, what should you do now to fill in the gaps until your bigger plan comes to fruition (possibly literally)?


An interesting thing about plants is that unlike most projects there is almost nothing you can do to speed things up.  You can buy plants that have already done some of their growing, and you can do this and that with fertilizer, but basically once you've planted something, it doesn't matter how many people you have or how much money you have, it's going to take as long as it takes.


So the first thing that *I* do is to visualize what I ultimately want: maybe a tree here, some roses there, maybe a small rockery and a water feature.  In very little time, I can imagine all kinds of possibilities.  With a little more effort, I can draw up a sketch of the available space, mark out which "zones" are going to have sunlight and which are going to be shady, and mark off areas and indicate possibilities in a vague way.  If I want, I can do this ten times over the course of a week.  And, as I do, I come down on "the sort of thing" that I want.  I can go to a garden centre and I can obtain ideas for what they have and what it's possible to plant and how big things will ultimately be.  All of this has cost me negligible money and taken almost no time.


Of course, I haven't actually *done* anything yet.


More importantly, do I have a "plan"?  No, I would say not.  I have an idea of a possible end state in five years' time.  In order to have anything resembling a plan, I need to figure out what I'm going to do, in what order, and when I'm going to do it.  And make sure that I don't end up destroying anything I've already done in order to take a later step.  For instance, I don't want to plant a tree in year 1 only to find two years later that I need to dig there to fit something else in.


I also need to be aware of how much everything is going to cost and spend the appropriate amount each year until the job is done.


Then it's just a question of doing it all.


What, I hear you ask, does this have to do with technology?  I'm glad you asked.


Well, when this occurred to me the other day, standing in my son's garden, it occurred to me that this is basically no different from the way I plan anything else - mainly software projects. There are differences of course - in a software project you can control variables such as budget and staff to deliver results more quickly.  And you need to keep at a software project - if you abandon a garden it will keep growing (although maybe not exactly as you had in mind).


The first thing I do when starting on a project is to mull it over - possibly by myself if it's a personal project, or with clients and stakeholders if it's a contract.  I know a lot of people who think this "takes a long time" and are very keen to start "on the real work", but on the scale of things, much like pondering a garden, it's almost free.  You can think things through while doing other things, and you can talk things through on a regular basis - maybe for an hour once a week - while "working" on other things.  I always find long walks to be the most efficient time for mulling over current or future projects - or indeed articles for my website.


Once I feel we have a general concept of what we are trying to achieve, I try and nail it down with sketches, or scripts or spreadsheets.  Whatever feels like it would be an input or an output from the actual application.  As we do this, it flushes out aspects of the requirements and design that can easily be missed in a more abstract discussion.  I always try to start with the "key" features of the software - what will users do _most_ frequently?  How will they navigate?  Am I forcing them to jump through hoops?


Now it's time to move on the next stage - can we talk about design?  What would a minimally viable product look like?  How do we get it to our users?  What features does it *have* to have?  What corners can we cut?  How can we build our user base most expeditiously?  What can we do in parallel?


And when we have all that, we have something that _looks like_ a plan.  But, as with the garden, it is not set in stone.  It's important to consider what will trip us up later if we don't take it into account now - there is no use setting off to build the first version of something that will ultimately need to interact with a third-party system in a language that cannot interact with that.  If there are risks that we are taking with the design, can we incorporate some aspect of that in the MVP?  If not, can we do a small experiment around that?


After that, it's just a question of building things - and iterating.  A few features here and there, deliver to users, gain user feedback, turn that into new ideas - until we are told that's enough.


And then we stop.
