Iteration is one of those things that comes naturally to me, so naturally that it is a revelation that it doesn't seem that way to other people.  Many people see me as a "planner" - which is true, but with the caveat that I only want to plan a little way ahead.  I don't, for example, want to set out for a day out by train only to turn up at the train station to find I've missed a train or that the whole line is closed for the day.  I know - and indeed, admire - people who can immediately pivot to doing something else and chalk it up as "serendipity".  On the other hand, I do not need to come up with a precise schedule of the days activities, nor do I need to plan my trips out six months in advance.  The phrase "just in time" almost applies, except I feel I need a bit more buffer than that, because I don't trust "just".


Since I'm talking about things technical here, I'll bring it back to work.  I've done a lot of software development, and the only time it has seemed "unnatural" is when I've worked for big companies with big, long term plans where I'm being asked to work on something that delivers no immediate value.  It may or may not be clear what the "it" is that you are trying to produce, but there is always one thing that is clear: you have three months in which to do "it".  I now refuse to work on such projects (by the simple expedient of only taking contract work for a timeframe of about 3-6 months, usually to try an get something "over the line").


There is something totally natural to me about the rhythm of the week.  I don't know what it is, but I suspect that my appreciation of that _exact_ timeframe was settled at school: on Monday, I went to school refreshed and ready to go; by Friday I was an empty husk who "couldn't go on a moment longer"; Friday evening was a time to clear up the week (getting homework out of the way, etc), reflect and chill out.  Saturday was "my" day, where I could do anything I wanted; and Sunday was a generally more structured, family day.  And repeat.


I see the cycle of the seasons over the course of the year, and I understand that months go by; but neither of those have the same rhythm to me: a year is just too long to be an interesting cycle; and months in Western culture are just completely detached from anything (the only ritual we have to mark their passing is flicking over a calendar).


The only other rhythm I feel in life is the day, and in particular the weekday has a rhythm which in some sense is a microcosm of the week: get up, apply myself, be worn out, reflect, relax, sleep.


So it's very natural to me to see a week as the right unit of work for a software development process.  When you turn up to work on Monday morning, it should be a blank slate: you've had the weekend off, you should have recharged and while you might be thinking about work, you should _not_ be thinking about "how do I fix this on Monday" but rather a more holistic "how is the code doing?".  In other words, if you are going to be bringing _anything_ with you into the planning meeting, it is "the code needs this to be better" rather than "well, here is where we're up to".


Monday morning then sets the tone for the week ahead: this is what we are going to do, and this is how we are going to go about it.  There are four-plus days of solid work, delivering value for your client(s), and then at the end of the week all of the "wrap-up" activities: a show-and-tell to stakeholders who weren't there seeing it happen; a retrospective of how things went and what might be done differently next week; and a celebration that it all got done.


Even outside of work, I follow the same pattern: I have things in my personal life that need to be done, and, yes, I have a "backlog" of things that I maintain (in what I think of as my "client" role).  At the beginning of the week, I look at what commitments I have, make sure that those have priorities, and then attempt to fit in as many of the backlog items as I think need to fit.  Sometimes (if I need to make appointments, or coordinate with others), I will put them in specific timeslots, but in general I will put them in a rough order in which I think they will get done - priority, simplicity or convenience.  Every day, I review this plan and identify some that I want to do "today", and try to stick to that.  At the end of the week, I reflect on what happened, what gone done and what didn't, and why.  That knowledge goes back into the process by which I plan the following week.


This seems natural to me.  And it still seems odd that it doesn't to (many) other people.  I can't figure out if they're missing something or if I am.  Or if we are, really, all just that different.
